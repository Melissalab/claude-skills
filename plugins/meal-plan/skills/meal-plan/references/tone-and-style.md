# Tone and Style Guide

The voice of this document is the single most important thing that distinguishes it from a generic meal plan. The person reading it should feel like a thoughtful friend who knows their situation is sitting next to them explaining things. Not a coach yelling motivation, not a doctor reciting warnings, not a fitness influencer selling a transformation.

This guide collects the specific patterns that produce that voice, side by side with the patterns that break it.

## Addressing the reader

Always second person. Always "you" and "your." Never refer to the person in the third person after the cover page. Never use their name more than twice in the whole document (once on the cover, maybe once more in a closing line).

Good: "You'll notice the breakfasts alternate between eggs and Greek yogurt..."

Bad: "Chad will notice his breakfasts alternate..."

Bad: "The reader will find that breakfasts alternate..."

## Sentence construction

Friends speak in complete, flowing sentences connected by natural transitions. They do not speak in numbered rules, bullet-pointed orders, or bolded imperatives.

Good: "A handful of small habits make a bigger difference than any single meal choice. None of this is complicated, but getting these locked in is what actually moves your numbers."

Bad: "**RULE 1:** Kill liquid sugar. **RULE 2:** Flip packages. **RULE 3:** Protein always."

## Emphasis

Use bolded lead-ins at the start of a paragraph to introduce a topic, followed by prose explanation. Never use all capital letters for emphasis. All caps reads as shouting and instantly breaks the friendly voice.

Good: "**Cut out liquid sugar entirely.** Soda, sweet tea, juice, and flavored coffee creamer all hit your bloodstream faster than anything you could eat..."

Bad: "THE SINGLE BIGGEST LEVER. Zero liquid sugar is the fastest A1C drop."

Bad: "THIS IS CRITICAL — you must avoid liquid sugar AT ALL COSTS."

## Phrases to avoid

The following phrases should never appear in the document. They sound like marketing copy or coach-speak, and they break the voice immediately.

Avoid: "no-BS," "no-guesswork," "game-changer," "move the needle," "crush your A1C," "drop those numbers," "unlock," "transform," "the whole game," "mission-critical," "level up," "your playbook," "your blueprint," "the only tool you need," "this one trick."

Prefer instead: plain descriptive phrases that would sound natural in ordinary conversation. "The biggest thing you can do," "the main thing that helps," "what actually matters here," "the part that's easy to miss."

## Handling sensitive topics

Diabetes, A1C numbers, weight, and medication are all topics where clinical or alarmist language can make the reader feel judged. The document should treat these matter-of-factly without either minimizing the health issue or dramatizing it.

Good: "Since diabetes often comes with medications or other things going on that affect how you eat, it's always worth running any big changes past your doctor first."

Bad: "WARNING: If you are on diabetes medication, consult your physician before making dietary changes, as hypoglycemic events may result."

Bad: "Your high A1C is putting you at serious risk of complications — this plan is your lifeline."

## The closing caveat

Every document ends with a short italic paragraph positioned as a friendly afterthought. It acknowledges that everyone reacts differently to food, points toward the Section 6 reference list for swaps, mentions checking with a doctor for big changes since other medications may interact, and frames the whole document as a friendly starting point rather than medical advice. The tone is "oh, one last thing" rather than "please read this important disclaimer."

Good closing: "One last thought: everyone's body works a little differently. If there are foods you react to or just don't tolerate well, swap them out freely using the reference list in Section 4. And since diabetes often comes with medications or other things going on that affect how you eat, it's always worth running any big changes past your doctor first. They know the full picture in a way a weekly meal plan never can. Think of this as a friendly starting point, not medical advice."

## Typography

Do not use em dashes (—) or en dashes (–) anywhere in the document. This is a deliberate typographic choice. Substitute as follows.

For label-to-value relationships in lists and notes, use a colon: "Sharp cheddar: 8 oz" (not "Sharp cheddar — 8 oz").

For brief parenthetical asides within a sentence, use commas around the aside: "This plan sits just slightly below maintenance, which is a gentle way to support better blood sugar, without losing weight."

For joining two complete thoughts that would normally take an em dash, use a period and start a new sentence: "That one habit catches most of the traps. 'Honey BBQ' wings and 'teriyaki' anything are all sugar wearing a meat costume."

For number ranges, spell out "to": "22 to 25 minutes" (not "22–25 minutes"), "10 to 12 wings" (not "10–12 wings"), "about 6 to 7 lbs of meat" (not "6–7 lbs").

## Specificity

Use concrete numbers, brand names, and practical details over vague generalities. Friends give you actual information, not advice that sounds like it came from a magazine.

Good: "Grab a cheap glucose meter. They run about twenty dollars at Kroger. Test before you eat and two hours after, for just one week."

Bad: "Consider monitoring your blood glucose levels periodically to better understand your body's response to various foods."

## Length and pacing

Paragraphs in the friend voice tend to run four to six sentences. Long enough to develop a thought with real substance, short enough that the reader does not lose the thread. One-sentence paragraphs work as occasional emphasis but should not pile up. The pacing should feel like someone who has thought about this carefully and is taking the time to explain it properly, without rushing or padding.
